Case Study 03 / Personal · iOS app

A reading app worthy of the stories you love.

Verso brings AO3 works, EPUBs, and pasted text into one library and reads them aloud, word by word, with the screen locked. It started as a React Native prototype and shipped as a native SwiftUI app — because the feature it was built around could not survive the abstraction.

TL;DR

A solo project: a reader for people whose library is scattered across AO3 tabs, EPUB files, and pasted drafts. Three ways in, three typographic themes crossed with four luminance modes, and text-to-speech with word-level highlighting that keeps playing on the lock screen. The interesting part is the architecture: narration was prototyped on expo-speech, outgrew it, was replaced by a hand-written native module, and eventually took the whole app native. Android was deferred rather than shipped degraded.

01

Three ways in, one shelf

People who read a lot of fiction do not keep it in one place. Some of it is on AO3 behind a dozen browser tabs, some is an EPUB in Files or Drive, some is text pasted out of a document. Verso's premise is that all of it belongs on the same shelf.

So the Add Story sheet opens on three tabs — AO3 Link, Paste Text, File — rather than making one path primary and burying the others. Paste an AO3 URL and title, author, tags, and every chapter come across from the work page; there is no clipping, no per-chapter fetching, no browser round-trip.

Each story on the shelf carries the metadata a reader actually triages by: word count, chapter count, content rating, and when it was last updated.

02

Themes and modes are two different questions

Most readers collapse "how it looks" into a single list: light, dark, sepia, maybe a black-on-white accessibility option. Verso splits it in two, because they are not the same decision.

Theme is typographic character — Folio, the classic; Hush, warm and quiet; Codex, high-contrast and crisp. Mode is luminance — Light, Sepia, Dark, and HC, plus System to follow the phone. Choosing a serif you like should not force you into a brightness you don't.

That gives twelve explicit combinations rather than four presets, and it puts high contrast on the luminance axis where it belongs — an accessibility mode available in any theme, not a separate theme that overrides your typographic choice.

Font size, line height, and margins are steppers rather than sliders — a reader setting these once wants a value they can return to, not one they have to re-find by dragging.

03

When the feature decides the architecture

Verso's narration is not a bolt-on. The whole point is that you can put the phone in your pocket mid-sentence and keep going — so the text-to-speech has to survive backgrounding, respond to the lock screen, and highlight the word currently being spoken so your eyes can rejoin the page exactly where your ears are.

That last requirement is the one that broke the stack. Word-level highlighting needs word boundary callbacks from the speech synthesiser, and the cross-platform speech API did not expose them — along with reliable pause/resume and mid-utterance rate changes.

  1. Phase 1
    Prototype on expo-speech

    Fast to stand up, enough to prove the reading experience. Audio session configured to play in silent mode and stay active in the background.

  2. Phase 2
    Hit the ceiling, write a native module

    Pause/resume, mid-rate-change, and word boundary callbacks were all out of reach. Replaced with a hand-written Expo module wrapping AVSpeechSynthesizer directly, with an interruption observer so calls and Siri pause narration and it resumes when permitted.

  3. Cost
    The cross-platform benefit stops paying

    A custom native module cannot run in Expo Go, so testing moved to custom dev builds. The abstraction was now something to work around on the one feature that mattered most.

  4. Android
    Defer parity rather than ship degraded

    The obvious shortcut was to let Android fall back to the generic speech API — shipping two platforms, one of them worse. Rejected: Android was deferred until it could have a real native module of its own, and the JS facade was designed so the reader would need no changes when it landed.

  5. Shipped
    Rebuilt in SwiftUI, iPhone-only

    The React Native prototype was archived and Verso was rewritten natively — SwiftUI, AVFoundation, MediaPlayer, no third-party dependencies at all. Narration continues with the device locked, with Now Playing controls and ±15s skip on the lock screen.

This React Native/Expo prototype is no longer the Verso app. Verso was rebuilt natively in SwiftUI. Nothing here ships. Kept for history only.

— the archived prototype's README, 18 August 2026

What the rewrite bought

Word-synced highlighting that holds up at every speed, background playback that behaves like a podcast app, and a dependency list of exactly zero third-party packages.

What it cost was Android, and a second build of an app that already worked. Both were the price of the feature the app exists for.

04

Teaching around a platform limit

iOS ships with a thin set of default voices. The good ones — Premium and Enhanced — are free, but they live four levels deep in Settings and most people never learn they exist.

An app can respond to this three ways: pretend the default voices are fine, bundle its own voices at significant size and licensing cost, or tell the truth. Verso tells the truth. The voice picker names the limitation, explains that better voices sound dramatically more natural, and gives the exact path — Settings → Accessibility → Spoken Content → Voices — noting that anything downloaded appears here automatically.

Every voice also has a preview button, so the difference is audible before it is committed to a chapter.

It is a small piece of copy doing work that no amount of interface polish could: the honest version is more useful than the seamless one.

05

No account, no algorithm

Verso has no sign-in, no analytics, and no recommendation engine. Your library is on your device; reading progress and settings never leave it. The only time the app touches the network is when you hand it an AO3 URL and ask it to fetch a work.

Narration runs entirely on-device through the system speech synthesiser, so listening to a story is not a request to anybody's server. After import, the app works fully offline.

  • AccountNone. Nothing to sign in to.
  • AnalyticsNone. No SDK, no events, no identifiers.
  • Third-party depsZero. SwiftUI, AVFoundation, MediaPlayer only
  • NetworkOnly to fetch an AO3 work the reader asks for
  • NarrationOn-device speech synthesis, no network
  • StorageLocal only — library, progress, and settings
  • AdsNone

Verso is an unofficial reader for AO3, not affiliated with or endorsed by Archive of Our Own, and imported works remain the property of their authors. Because AO3 import lets a reader bring in adult material, the app is rated 17+ — a rating chosen for what users can import, not for anything Verso ships.

06

Rejected once, for the right reason

Build 1.0 (4) was rejected under Guideline 2.5.4: the app declared the background audio capability, but review could not get any audible content to play in the background.

The audio pipeline was fine. The bug was in the reviewer's path through the app — sample content had been compiled in under #if DEBUG, so a Release build launched to an empty shelf. With nothing on the shelf there was nothing to narrate, and the capability looked unjustified.

The fix was to ship the sample story in every build, so the first launch always has something to read, plus review notes with a numbered path to background playback and a screen recording from a physical device. Build 1.0 (5) went through.

It is a good reminder that a feature is not shipped until a stranger can find it. The empty first-run state was invisible in development, where the sample content had always been there.
